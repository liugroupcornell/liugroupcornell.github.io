Calcium catalysed nucleophilic fluorination

Original Paper | Inorganic chemistry | 2026-11-18 19:00 EST

Job J. C. Struijs, Agamemnon E. Crumpton, Véronique Gouverneur, Simon Aldridge

New catalytic manifolds that challenge existing boundaries for synthesis and sustainable manufacturing are notoriously difficult to implement1, particularly for catalytic paradigms based on metals which lack access to the fundamental processes that underpin many established d-block mechanistic pathways (e.g. oxidative addition and reductive elimination). The widespread exploitation of systems based on main-group elements therefore requires alternative elementary steps to effect turnover. Herein, we demonstrate a new catalytic platform for nucleophilic fluorination, which exploits a complex based on calcium (the fifth most Earth-abundant element) as the fluoride delivery vehicle. The key intermediate is a calcium-fluoride complex featuring a terminal Ca-F interaction, a unique structural geometry enabled by a highly sterically encumbered dianionic ligand-scaffold. This intermediate links reactants and products through F- capture and release steps, and thereby unlocks catalytic F- shuttling. Late Ca-F bond breaking in the rate-determining fluoride-delivery transition-state means that electronic and steric tuning of F- by calcium is chemically significant; proof-of-concept results show a hitherto unprecedented level of selectivity for substitution over elimination brought about by Ca-modulation. In a broader context, this work demonstrates how control over catalyst speciation and reagent binding affinity are key factors for the future development of sustainable non-transition metal paradigms in catalysis.

The retention of nanoparticles in solid tumours

Original Paper | Nanotechnology in cancer | 2026-11-18 19:00 EST

Atta C. Y. Chang, Luan N. M. Nguyen, Qin Ji, Colin Blackadar, Stefan M. Mladjenovic, Warren C. W. Chan

The active transport and retention principle states that nanoparticles enter tumours via endothelial cells and exit via lymphatic drainage. How nanoparticles are retained in the tumour microenvironment remains unclear. Here we report a computational strategy to determine the mechanism of nanoparticle retention in solid tumours. We find that tumour cellular and acellular components delay and obstruct the movement of nanoparticles by influencing nanoparticle drag, Brownian and electric forces. We mathematically define nanoparticle retention based on these interactions, linking retention quantitatively to tumour pathophysiology, nanoparticle design and delivery efficiency. We create a software interface to simulate and predict nanoparticle retention across different tumour systems and nanoparticle designs, enabling us to select optimal tumour and nanoparticle delivery conditions for better therapeutic outcomes.

Photonic Ising machines towards and beyond a million spins

Combinatorial optimization problems are central to many challenges in logistics, finance, engineering and the life sciences, but they remain computationally demanding. Many of these problems can be mapped onto the Ising model, in which binary spins interact through a network of couplings, and solutions correspond to low-energy, ideally ground-state, spin configurations. Photonic Ising machines have the potential to be fast and energy-efficient heuristic solvers of optimization problems by leveraging the low latency, high bandwidth and inherent parallelism of optics. However, current photonic implementations remain limited in scalability, connectivity, reconfigurability and time-to-solution, which prevents their use in many practical applications. In this Review, we examine the current landscape of photonic Ising machines, discuss the challenges and limitations of existing platforms and identify the scientific and technological advances needed to realize large-scale systems.

Quiet within, chaos without: Turbulence shelters drive selective particle capture and release

Research Article | Fluid dynamics | 2026-11-19 03:00 EST

Matteo Sabato, Sarah Chong, Yaocheng Li, Zhe Xu, Daqian Gao, Martin J. Falk, Stefano Sacanna, William T. M. Irvine

Turbulence is chaotic, yet its fluctuations follow a structured, nonequilibrium energy cascade unconstrained by the equilibrium theorems that limit passive energy extraction from molecular gases. Here, we introduce turbulence shelters: hollow shells with a single aperture that filter eddies by scale, sustaining gradients in fluctuation intensity that drive directional particle transport despite zero mean fluid flux. Across three orders of magnitude in size, shelters establish particle concentration contrasts between their interior and the surrounding flow, with selectivity controlled by particle geometry, inertia, thermal fluctuations, and electrostatic forces. At the microscale, shelters discriminate particles differing by tens of nanometers independently of material composition. These results establish a design principle for selective filtration: Geometry that compartmentalizes turbulent fluctuations can harness ambient eddies to direct particle transport.
