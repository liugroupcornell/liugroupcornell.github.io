Strain-transfer at the Cu nanoparticle-Si(111) interface: static atomistic modelling and simulated Bragg CDI

New Submission | Materials Science (cond-mat.mtrl-sci) | 2026-11-24 20:00 EST

Corentin Chatelier

Substrate strain offers a route to control deformation in supported metallic nanoparticles, provided that interfacial coupling transmits the imposed loading into the particle. We assess this feasibility using five static Cu/Si(111) configurations spanning 0-2% biaxial tensile substrate strain. A 211,288-atom Cu particle is supported by a nominally 50 x 50 x 20 nm Si slab with its bottom 1 nm fixed, using the Jelinek modified embeddedatom potential. Relative to the relaxed supported 0% state, Cu-only local fits reveal heterogeneous, anisotropic deformation: particle-wide mean hydrostatic strain is negative at every nonzero loading, from -3.12 x 10-6 to -8.84 x 10-6, while the upper-particle mean varies nonmonotonically. Simulated Bragg CDI of Cu(111), obtained by inversion of known complex atomic scattering amplitudes, maps loading-dependent projected displacement and strain. The combined atomistic and Bragg CDI representations provide strong preliminary support for the feasibility of substrate-mediated strain transfer in this idealized system and identify spatial signatures for experimental investigation. Experiments remain indispensable to confirm transfer in real Cu/Si(111) nanoparticles and determine its magnitude.

From raw magnetoresistance to reliable cyclotron masses: A practical guide to processing quantum oscillation data

New Submission | Strongly Correlated Electrons (cond-mat.str-el) | 2026-11-24 20:00 EST

Josh Leeman, Sudipta Chatterjee, Max Hirschberger, Leslie M. Schoop

Quantum oscillations are an essential probe of the Fermi surface in conductors, and cyclotron masses extracted using the Lifshitz-Kosevich (LK) formula are used to evaluate correlation strength and the assignment of topological states. These masses depend on choices in signal processing, including the field window, method of background subtraction, and spectral window applied before the Fourier transform. These choices are often guided by visual inspection, yet they can alter spectral features and bias the extracted masses. We show that (1) representing a finite field interval by a single effective field can underestimate the cyclotron mass; (2) polynomial background subtraction can attenuate and shift low-frequency peaks; and (3) spectral tapering can reduce mass bias from leakage between neighboring peaks, at the cost of frequency resolution. We demonstrate these effects using numerical calculations and Shubnikov-de Haas experiments on the rare-earth tritellurides RTe3 (R = Pr, Nd, Gd, Tb) with a dense low-frequency spectrum in the charge ordered state. The examples reveal masses shifting by tens of percent with the field window, apparent peak shifts produced only by the background fit, and a reported departure from LK behavior that resolves into two neighboring frequencies.

New Submission | Materials Science (cond-mat.mtrl-sci) | 2026-11-24 20:00 EST

Peikun Zhang, Hongbo Zhang, Ying Xu, Zhuhua Zhang

Interactions at solid-liquid interfaces are fundamental to hydrovoltaic and catalytic technologies. While carbon nanomaterials have been extensively studied, the role of abundant defects in amorphous carbon remains poorly understood. Here, using first-principles calculations, we systematically investigate how various defects (topological, point, line, edge, and doping defects) modulate water-carbon interactions. We find that armchair-edge defects with dangling bonds reduce the energy barrier for water dissociation by up to 30 times compared with pristine carbon, while di-vacancies exhibit a near-zero Gibbs free energy for hydrogen evolution, enabling potential hydrogen evolution reaction activity on a purely carbon-based interface. Crucially, we demonstrate that coupling distinct defects produces synergistic effects that outperform individual defects, revealing a promising strategy for designing multifunctional interfaces. These insights establish a defect-engineering framework for the rational design of high-efficiency hydrovoltaic and catalytic devices.

Several different experiments have found apparent temperature differences of up to 10 K between the interior of mitochondria and the rest of the cellular environment. The physics of heat conduction, however, precludes the steady-state existence of a temperature difference greater than the microkelvin scale within the cell, absent a far greater energy budget. Nearly a decade after the first reported measurement, this apparent paradox remains unexplained. For $ N_f=8$ flavors the Mott transition at $ \nu=-2$ occurs at $ s^2_c\approx0.025$ , where the top of the lower Hubbard band reaches the Fermi level and the local moments are Kondo screened, forming a heavy Fermi liquid (HFL). The HFL extends to the hole-doped side, $ \nu=-2-x$ , but above the small Kondo temperature $ T_K$ the state is an incoherent bad metal without a Fermi surface. At low temperature, an anti-Hund’s coupling $ J_A\sim0.1U$ replaces the HFL with a pseudogap metal that has a small Fermi surface and is dominated by spin-polaron excitations at small $ x$ . We propose this pseudogap metal as the parent state of superconductivity in TBG. The framework may also be applied to other topological bands, such as those of twisted WSe$ _2$ .

Ambient-pressure superconductivity in bulk single crystals of La5Ni4O10

Physical Sciences - Article | Superconducting properties and materials | 2026-11-23 19:00 EST

Pascal Puphal, Runze Zhang, Pascal Reiss, Alexandra Krajewska, Masahiko Isobe, HIDENORI TAKAGI

Superconductivity in the Ruddlesden-Popper (RP) nickelates has so far been confined either to multilayer compounds under high pressure or to square-planar infinite-layer-type films stabilized by epitaxial strain. Here, we report the high-pressure high-temperature (HPHT) growth of bulk single crystals of the quadruple-layer RP nickelate La5Ni4O13 (n=4) and reproduce the quintuple-layer La6Ni5O16 (n = 5) synthesis, phases previously accessible only as thin films. Topotactic reduction of La5Ni4O13 single crystals with CaH2 yields the square-planar La5Ni4O10, which we show to be a bulk, ambient-pressure superconductor with Tonset c ≈ 18.7 K, a shielding fraction above 60%, and a specific-heat jump that confirms a bulk pairing transition. In transport, all crystals show an anomaly near 90 K reminiscent of a density-wave like transition, which may arise from a partially gapped Fermi surface or from the non-superconducting volume fraction. The upper critical field shows a small anisotropy, γ ≈ 2.4, which remains finite as T →Tc, indicating three-dimensional superconductivity with substantial coupling between the four-layer blocks. These results identify the square-planar nickelates Rn+1NinO2n+2 as a bulk platform in which the layer number alone tunes the nickel valence into the superconducting regime, without chemical substitution, pressure, or epitaxial constraint.

3D Snapshot of Ultrafast Terahertz Electromagnetic Wave Packets

Article | Terahertz optics | 2026-11-23 19:00 EST

Zhengyan Li, Yaodan Hu, Yisen Zhang, Jiapeng Li, Fan Xia, Changda Peng, Kuanjun Fan, Ping Li

Optics and photonics study the sciences of electromagnetic waves, which were well formulated by Maxwell’s equations more than 160 years ago. However, surprisingly, the fundamental problem of experimentally measuring an electromagnetic wave with all necessary electric field information in one go is yet to be realized. Instead, only limited physical quantities describing an optical wave can be picked out and measured, such as intensity, phase, spectrum, polarization, and their spatial or/and temporal profile. Here we demonstrate a single-shot three-dimensional (3D) spatiotemporal visualization of an electromagnetic wave packet in the terahertz (THz) spectral regime, unveiling complete spatiotemporal properties of light, including the three complete electric field components Ex, Ey and Ez, and their carrier-envelope phases. To capture such a 3D snapshot, the THz wave packet information is imprinted in a whole on a chirped ultrafast near-infrared probe laser pulse through electro-optic sampling, and extracted from the optical probe via a proposed technique of single-shot full-Stokes compressive spatiospectral polarimetry. 3D snapshots of electromagnetic wave packets not only solve the fundamental problem of seeing the traveling wave solution to Maxwell’s equations, but also open the door to holistic optical wave sensing.
