Where heat flows and where to change it: gauge-free sensitivity maps of thermal conductivity in disordered solids

New Submission | Materials Science (cond-mat.mtrl-sci) | 2026-11-06 20:00 EST

D. A. Drabold, C. Ugwumadu

Designing materials for thermal management requires knowing where heat flows, not only how much. Within harmonic (Allen$ –$ Feldman) theory we develop two local descriptions and test them on amorphous silicon. The first, a projection of the conductivity $ \kappa$ onto atoms, depends on how the potential energy is divided among atoms (a “gauge”); we find the dependence moderate, 23% of the map’s spatial standard deviation at single sites and 9.5% over regions of radius 0.4 nm, apart from a uniform offset of 0.8% of $ \kappa$ . The second avoids the division altogether. It is the exact response of $ \kappa$ to the mass of each atom and the stiffness of each bond, it obeys an Euler sum rule, and it contains the heat-flow map as one of three exact terms. In amorphous and paracrystalline silicon, with ACE, GAP-18 and Keating force constants, the two maps are anti-correlated: where little heat flows a heavier atom lowers $ \kappa$ , and where much heat flows it tends to raise it. The explanation is local softness. The harmonic vibrational amplitude of each atom predicts both maps, and at room temperature the anti-correlation largely disappears at fixed amplitude, in a 32,768-atom replica as well; below about 100 K a component remains that softness does not capture. Thus soft regions, which carry little heat, act through the mixing of vibrational modes about 1$ –$ 2 meV apart. In paracrystalline silicon the grains conduct more heat per atom, while the disordered matrix controls the response. Exact recalculations show that the response map guides the placement of dispersed substituents and locates the region that limits transport across an amorphous/crystalline interface.

Stability and Relaxation of Electron-Phonon Systems at Strong Coupling

New Submission | Strongly Correlated Electrons (cond-mat.str-el) | 2026-11-06 20:00 EST

Noga Bashan, Erez Berg

We study the kinetic properties of Migdal-Eliashberg theory (MET) for electron-phonon interactions at strong coupling. Previously, the system was found to be dynamically unstable at large couplings within a Markovian quantum Boltzmann approximation. Using the quasiclassical Kadanoff-Baym equation, we show that this instability is absent when non-Markovian effects are taken into account. We also analyze the long-time relaxation dynamics at strong-couplin, and find a clear imprint of the non-Markovian nature of the dynamics: in an intermediate temperature range between the Debye frequency and the coupling strength, the relaxation rate becomes linear in temperature with Planckian slope, while the leading relaxation mode acquires a large imaginary component, producing strongly oscillatory decay. Our results imply that any instability which preempts superconductivity must arise from effects beyond MET, such as phonon softening or polaronic effects at finite electronic bandwidth. This shows that the characteristic phonon frequency is not a fundamental bound on $ T_c$ , and leaves open a route to higher $ T_c$ in multi-orbital electron-phonon systems.

Alexandre Chaduteau, Hao Chen, Juraj Hasik, Titus Neupert

Symmetry-protected topological (SPT) phases are the simplest example of quantum phases that cannot be detected by local order parameters. Their classification is by now well-established from multiple perspectives, a prominent one being via tensor network states. The practical question, however, is not mere classification, but detection: given the ground state of an interacting Hamiltonian and a symmetry group $ G$ along with its representation, is there an efficient way to tell if the state is in a SPT phase with respect to $ G$ ? While there exist such practical tests in one spatial dimension (1D), methods in 2D have so far been restricted to finite-size systems or require an explicit form of virtual symmetries of the tensor networks representing the ground state. Moreover, it is efficient as it does not require finite-size scalings: it works directly in the thermodynamic limit. We numerically demonstrate the procedure by mapping out phase boundaries away from fixed-point wavefunctions. We extract the SPT label cocycle data explicitly from the PEPS tensor.

Topological Transitions of Evanescent Waves

New Submission | Mesoscale and Nanoscale Physics (cond-mat.mes-hall) | 2026-11-06 20:00 EST

Ignacio Pavez, Tom Sheppard, Yoonseok Hwang, Hannah M. Price, Frank Schindler

We study the band structure of evanescent waves, that is, the decaying or growing solutions that live within the gaps of tight-binding Hamiltonians. Although the underlying Hamiltonian is Hermitian, these modes carry complex wavevectors and therefore exhibit a non-Hermitian dispersion. Using the transfer matrix formalism in one dimension, we show that, in addition to the standard exceptional points that appear at the band edges, a different type of exceptional point can arise inside the gap, protected by spinless time-reversal symmetry. Tuning model parameters drives a topological transition where such in-gap exceptional points are nucleated in pairs, mediated by a crossing between purely imaginary momentum branches. We show that this spectral structure is diagnosed by a $ \mathbb{Z}_2$ index assigned to every energy inside the gap, with the in-gap exceptional points arising as its domain walls. A generic bound state in the system has a tail that is purely exponential when its energy lies between a band edge and the nearest exceptional point, but becomes oscillatory - with a wavevector differing from the band-edge momentum - when its energy lies between the pair of in-gap exceptional points. We illustrate these results using continuum and tight-binding models, as well as an anisotropic photonic crystal.

Topological defects in nematic liquid crystals are high-energy sites of reduced order. We show that a point defect can be stabilized in pillbox-shaped pores. The pore shape promotes lock-and-key docking similar to that observed for colloidal particles. The defect docks in one lobe or the other, displaying bistability. Furthermore, the defect moves like a charged object in an external electric field. An in-plane field drives the defect from one lobe to the opposite lobe, where it remains once the field is removed. Arrays of topological bits store and rewrite binary information in defect positions, offering a route to reconfigurable, field-addressable soft materials.
