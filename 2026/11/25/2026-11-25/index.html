An in situ redox-converted molecule for all-perovskite tandem solar cells

Original Paper | Solar cells | 2026-11-24 19:00 EST

Shaomin Yang, Chunxiang Lan, Miaomiao Zeng, Liqing Wu, Tian Zhang, Mimi Fu, Boyu Yang, Ziwei Zheng, Yu Wu, Jing Guo, Rui Guo, Mi Xu, Rongyan Wen, Zaiwei Wang, Shuangyan Hu, Mostafa Boshta, Jun Chen, Yaoguang Rong, Xiong Li

All-perovskite tandem solar cells (TSCs) are gaining prominence due to their ability to surpass the efficiency limit of single-junction perovskite solar cells (PSCs)1,2. However, the efficiency and stability of all-perovskite TSCs are limited by chemical instability at the hole transport layer/perovskite interface in the tin-lead (Sn-Pb) narrow-bandgap subcell, which introduces deep trap states from tin cations and promotes non-radiative recombination3-5. Here we incorporate a redox-converted molecule of p-aminobenzohydrazide (ABH) into poly(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene):polystyrene sulfonate (PEDOT:PSS) that neutralizes PSS acidity, heals Sn4+ defects, and generates a dipole passivation effect upon oxidation. During the preparation of Sn-Pb perovskites, ABH suppresses interfacial oxidation by reducing Sn4+ defects and converts to p-aminobenzaldehyde (ABD), which suppresses photogenerated hole-induced Sn2+ oxidation, aligning energy levels for efficient hole extraction. The in situ redox-converted molecule stabilizes the buried interface during both device fabrication and operation, reduces non-radiative recombination and enhances stability. The resulting narrow-bandgap Sn-Pb PSC achieves a power conversion efficiency (PCE) of 25.4% (certified 25.2%) with an open-circuit voltage of 0.92 V and a fill factor of 83.4%. Integrated into monolithic all-perovskite TSCs, the device delivers a PCE of 31.1% (certified 30.8%) together with enhanced thermal (ISOS-D-2I, 85 °C) and operational stability (ISOS-L-1I) under inert conditions.

An enduring climatic burden from past peatland drainage

Original Paper | Carbon cycle | 2026-11-24 19:00 EST

Jevan B. Yu, Etienne Fluet-Chouinard, Susan E. Page, Chris D. Evans, Charles F. Harvey, César Terrer

Over millennia, peatlands have removed hundreds of gigatonnes of carbon dioxide (CO2) from the atmosphere but emitted a proportion back as methane (CH4), exerting a slow net cooling effect1. Artificial drainage of these ecosystems–a centuries-old practice2,3–causes decomposition and the re-release of CO2 but suppresses CH4 emissions4, with a net climatic effect that is not well quantified at the global scale5. Here we reconstructed the radiative forcing from worldwide peatland drainage over 1800-2020 by combining a new flux dataset, hydrologically driven models of CO2 and CH4 fluxes and a hindcast of drainage extent and intensity. We found that drainage has produced a net heating effect of +73 (48-99) mW m-2–equivalent to 2.7 (1.8-3.6)% of industrial-era radiative forcing–originating from 0.4-0.5% of global land area. The net heating results from the dominant forcing of drainage-induced CO2 emissions (+83 (52-110) mW m-2) over that of avoided CH4 emissions (-9 (-15–2) mW m-2). Even without further expansion of drainage, maintaining currently drained areas could almost double the cumulative warming by 2100. Our reconstruction captures the opposing impacts of peatland drainage on global budgets of CO2 and CH4 and quantifies the strong legacy effects of historical drainage on present and possibly future climate.

A gut-brain sensory-motor circuit regulates sugar ingestion in Drosophila

Original Paper | Feeding behaviour | 2026-11-24 19:00 EST

Xinyue Cui
(崔心玥), Matthew R. Meiselman, Staci N. Thornton, Sophia Gustin, Jennifer Jeter, Geoffrey W. Meissner, Nilay Yapici

Bidirectional communication between the brain and digestive tract is critical for optimizing nutrient preference and food intake, yet the neural mechanisms that couple gut nutrient sensing with ingestive motor control remain poorly understood. Here we identify a gut-brain sensory-motor circuit that regulates sugar ingestion in flies. We show that the brain neurons that regulate food ingestion, IN1s1, receive excitatory input from enteric sensory neurons that penetrate the foregut epithelium and detect sugars in the gut lumen during ingestion. Functional imaging and connectome analyses reveal that IN1s integrate excitatory input from multiple sugar-sensing neurons, including enteric sensory neurons, and provide inhibitory output to enteric motor neurons that control the muscles at the entry of the crop–a stomach-like organ. During ingestion, IN1 activation suppresses these enteric motor neurons, relaxes the crop duct and promotes rapid transfer of food into the crop, thereby enabling sustained food intake. Together, our findings describe a gut-brain circuit that controls the motor activity of digestive tract muscles during feeding and suggest a circuit mechanism by which nutrient signals from the gut contribute to the dynamic regulation of food intake across metabolic states.

Majorana zero modes provide a route to fault-tolerant qubits by encoding information non-locally in fermion parity. Their noise sensitivity is expected to decrease exponentially with separation between Majorana zero modes, a suppression known as topological protection1. Kitaev chains in quantum-dot-superconductor arrays can host Majorana zero modes at their ends1,2, even in two-site chains3. These minimal-chain modes, known as poor man’s Majoranas, retain characteristic Majorana properties, including near-zero energy and equal electron-hole character, but lack topological protection3,4,5. Here we demonstrate coherent time-resolved rotations between parity states encoded by four poor man’s Majoranas in two coupled minimal Kitaev chains3,6,7,8. Our results establish coherent control of a qubit encoded in the fermion parity of Majorana zero modes, realized using minimal Kitaev chains. They lay the groundwork for universal qubit operations, comprehensive coherence characterization and probing protection in longer chains.

Here we analyse repeated measurements from 44,368 forest inventory plots spanning 1999-2025, with an average remeasurement interval of 10 years, to examine how climate and natural disturbances jointly influence the elevational redistribution of tree species across the western USA. We show that climate warming increased sapling abundance, whereas increasing drought frequency and recent natural disturbances (fire, insect outbreaks and disease) reduced tree abundance, particularly among adult trees. By contrast, stem abundance centres showed more divergent shifts, consistent with sapling responses to both climate change and disturbance. Our findings reveal contrasting demographic responses to climate change and natural disturbances that shape the elevational redistribution of different species, with potential consequences for forest composition, resilience and long-term stability.
