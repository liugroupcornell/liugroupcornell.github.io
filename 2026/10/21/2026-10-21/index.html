De novo design of enantioselective artificial metalloenzyme photocatalysts

Original Paper | Biocatalysis | 2026-10-20 20:00 EDT

Sandip Mishra, Payal, Declan Evans, Kingsley Bortey, Husayn Bootwala, Giovanni Gonzalez-Gutierrez, Ricardo Javier Vázquez, David Baker, Jared C. Lewis

The ability to create protein scaffolds that impart enantioselectivity to simple, readily available metal catalysts could redefine the landscape of asymmetric catalysis. Artificial metalloenzymes (ArMs), hybrid catalysts that combine a synthetic metal cofactor with a protein scaffold, offer a potential means to achieve this goal, but scaffold discovery has remained constrained by trial-and-error approaches and the functional limits of extant proteins.1-5 This challenge is acute for metal polypyridine photocatalysts, which are widely used in visible-light chemistry but have not enabled highly enantioselective photocatalysis within protein scaffolds. In this study, we show that generative protein design6-8 can create de novo scaffolds that selectively bind these photocatalysts and that directed evolution converts the resulting artificial metalloenzymes into efficient, highly enantioselective photocatalysts9. The evolved proteins support diverse light-driven reactions, high catalyst turnover and catalyst recycling, and can function with commercially available racemic metal complexes. Spectroscopic studies show that the protein environment extends the photocatalyst excited-state lifetime and organizes substrate near the cofactor, altering energy transfer and controlling stereoselectivity. These findings establish de novo protein design as a route to functional scaffolds for synthetic metal complexes that lack natural protein partners and provide a strategy for combining protein design and evolution to control non-natural catalytic chemistry.

The contribution of salt marshes to wave attenuation during extreme storms

Original Paper | Ecosystem services | 2026-10-20 20:00 EDT

Pim W. J. M. Willemsen, Victoria G. Mason, Vincent van Zelst, Vera M. van Bergeijk, Mark Klein Breteler, Jos R. M. Muller, Dimitrios Dermentzoglou, Akis Vouziouris, Thomas J. van Veelen, S. Dzimballa, Kathelijne M. Wijnberg, Antonius J. F. Hoitink, Paul Buring, Maike Paul, Alessandro Antonini, Tjeerd J. Bouma, Bas W. Borsje

Salt marshes protect coastlines by attenuating wind waves with their elevated marsh platform and above-ground vegetation1. Confidence in the persistence of this service under a changing climate requires knowledge on how extreme storms damage the platform and vegetation; however, a comprehensive, quantified understanding of salt marsh-wave interactions under extreme events is lacking. A unique experiment was conducted by transferring intact salt marsh to one of the world’s largest wave flumes, and subsequently exposing it to increasingly higher waves that reach up to 2 m significant wave height. We show that marsh substrates can persist with negligible erosion, despite cumulative wave exposure. By contrast, the vegetation was progressively damaged during continued wave exposure, gradually reducing vegetation-related wave attenuation. Results emphasize that vegetation was most effective in attenuating waves at low-energy marsh coasts (up to 1 m), where attenuation exceeded 10% (up to 50%), whereas attenuation at high-energy marsh coasts (wave height > 1 m) does not exceed 10%. Our global assessment supports the differentiation between low- and high-energetic conditions resulting from the flume experiment. Our results suggest that the marsh platform remains stable under extreme wave events and that substantial wave attenuation by marsh vegetation is limited to low-energy marsh coasts.

Zeolite-catalysed olefin carbonylation based on tuning spatial confinement

Original Paper | Catalytic mechanisms | 2026-10-20 20:00 EDT

Liang Qi, Yingying Sheng, Wenna Zhang, Youming Ni, Linhai He, Bin Li, Anjie Wang, Wenliang Zhu, Alexis T. Bell, Zhongmin Liu

Carbonylation is an important process used to produce aldehydes, carboxylic acids and esters1,2,3,4,5 that is usually catalysed by homogeneous metal complexes with ligands and iodide promoters6,7,8,9,10,11,12. Koch has reported that protons of mineral acids can also catalyse carbonylation13. Previous studies have demonstrated that Brønsted acidic zeolites can act as a heterogeneous, Koch-type catalyst for carbonylation of alcohols and olefins, but the products in this case are limited to tertiary carboxylic acids14,15,16,17,18. Here we show that targeted use of spatial confinement in a zeolite together with a high CO/olefin ratio can help break this limitation. We report that, with FER zeolite and a CO/olefin ratio of 360, olefin carbonylation can produce primary and secondary carboxylic acids as the main products; ethylene carbonylation to propanoic acid (PA) is achieved with 80% selectivity over 100 h and propylene carbonylation to isobutyric acid (IBA) is achieved with 95% selectivity over 750 h. The propylene and ethylene carbonylation selectivities decrease to 61% and 57%, respectively, as the CO/olefin ratio decreases to 24 and 90, respectively. Experiments in combination with theoretical calculations suggest that T2-O5 in eight-membered ring (8-MR) cavities of FER zeolite are the primary active sites for ethylene carbonylation and T3-O1 in ten-membered rings (10-MRs) are the primary active sites for propylene carbonylation. Our findings open new directions for olefin carbonylation and provide critical insights into the prerequisites for developing new zeolite-based carbonylation processes.

Cold activates a BH2 metabolic network promoting longevity and proteostasis

Original Paper | Mechanisms of disease | 2026-10-20 20:00 EDT

Hyun Ju Lee, Cansu Doğan, Seda Koyuncu, Markus Wehrmann, Murat Artan, Dunja Petrovic, Rute Loureiro, Amit Kumar Sahu, Joana Schnubel, Pavel Grobushkin, Dimitrios Prymidis, Theodoros Georgomanolis, Agnieszka Sokol, Ming Yang, Thorsten Hoppe, Christian Frezza, David Vilchez

Moderately cold temperatures extend lifespan and promote health across species1,2,3, yet the molecular mechanisms underlying these benefits remain unclear. Here we define a metabolite-driven, multi-tissue signalling system that links cold temperature to enhanced longevity and proteostasis in Caenorhabditis elegans. Cold temperature upregulates the biopterin-synthesis enzyme CAT-4 (GCH1 in humans), increasing production of dihydrobiopterin (BH2), a transportable reservoir for the bioactive cofactor tetrahydrobiopterin (BH4). BH2 is produced in muscle and transported to distal tissues, including the intestine and neurons, where it is converted into BH4 by the salvage enzyme DHFR-1. BH4 functions as an essential cofactor for phenylalanine hydroxylase PAH-1, promoting tyrosine synthesis and extending lifespan. This BH2-BH4 axis also prevents disease-related protein aggregation and subsequent neuronal dysfunction during ageing. Importantly, increasing BH2 synthesis specifically in muscle is sufficient to promote longevity and suppress pathological protein aggregation in distal tissues, even at normal or elevated temperatures. In human cells, BH2 or tyrosine supplementation reduces aggregation of polyQ-expanded proteins associated with Huntington’s disease, as well as mutant variants of TDP-43 and FUS that cause amyotrophic lateral sclerosis. Together, these findings identify BH2 as a cold-induced metabolite of which the synthesis, intertissue transport and conversion into bioactive BH4 preserve organism-wide proteostasis and extend lifespan.

WEE1 phosphorylates and inhibits the activity of cyclin-dependent kinases to prevent mitotic entry and establish a G2 DNA damage checkpoint1,2,3. Consequently, WEE1 inhibitors have been developed to be synthetically lethal in TP53-mutated tumours that become reliant on this WEE1-mediated checkpoint, and such inhibitors are undergoing clinical trials. These results reveal a mechanism of cell cycle control that has implications for how WEE1 inhibitors should be used in the clinic.

Anaerobic degradation of complex organic compounds to methane and carbon dioxide is common in the deep subsurface biosphere and has long been considered to require syntrophic cooperation, with methanogens confined to the terminal use of simple C1 and C2 substrates. Here we report the isolation of a thermophilic archaeon, Methanoglobus dolicholipophagus AA-75, affiliated with Archaeoglobi, from a Chinese petroleum reservoir. Plant development is highly plastic, with growth and architecture continuously shaped by environmental conditions. Roots, for instance, adjust their development to local water, nutrient and soil conditions. Despite this plasticity, individual root cell types are generally viewed as largely homogeneous populations that progress along a single developmental trajectory1. Here we show that rather than progressing along a single developmental trajectory, most Arabidopsis root cell lineage trajectories exhibit a stereotyped bifid topology, as revealed by single-cell RNA sequencing and spatial transcript mapping. One branch is characterized by a transcriptional program that is shared across multiple cell types and enriched for genes associated with environmental responses, including abscisic acid (ABA) signalling. Together, these findings reveal an additional layer of root cell identity that is superimposed on developmental lineage and its prevalence is modulated by environmental conditions. Angelman syndrome is a severe neurodevelopmental disorder caused by the loss of UBE3A, a gene that encodes an E3 ubiquitin ligase1,2.
