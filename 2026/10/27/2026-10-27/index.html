Direct imaging elucidates ionic memory in two-dimensional nanochannels

Original Paper | Nanofluidics | 2026-10-26 20:00 EDT

Kalluvadi Veetil Saurav, Nathan Ronceray, Baptiste Coquinot, Agustin D. Pizarro, Ashok Keerthi, Theo Emmerich, Aleksandra Radenovic, Boya Radha

Nanofluidic memristors promise brain-inspired information processing with ions, yet their microscopic origin remains debated. So far, ionic memory has been attributed to ion-specific interactions, dynamic wetting, chemical reactions or mechanical deformations, yet typically without direct experimental evidence. Here, by combining operando interferometric imaging with electrokinetic measurements, we directly visualize voltage-induced blistering of the confining walls of two-dimensional (2D) nanochannels as the key origin of memristive hysteresis. We identify two distinct classes of blisters: unidirectional, driven by electrostatic forces on surface charges, and bidirectional, arising from osmotic pressure due to concentration polarization. This mechanistic framework explains device evolution and device-to-device variability, and reframes stochastic blistering as a functional design element. Our results constitute a direct proof of electromechanical coupling as a robust pathway to ionic memory in 2D nanochannels and open routes towards high-performance ionic memristors and electrically actuated nanofluidic valves.

The Ising lattice gas and the surface diffusion of CO on Cu(001) at finite coverage

New Submission | Statistical Mechanics (cond-mat.stat-mech) | 2026-10-27 20:00 EDT

S. Miret-Artés

The memory equation of the coherent intermediate scattering function (ISF), derived previously for lattice gases, is applied to the Ising lattice gas under Kawasaki dynamics. The ISF follows the decay of a density wave of adparticles, a superposition of exponential relaxation modes: the decay starts at a rate fixed by a sum rule and is then slowed by a memory. The static structure factor and the effective hop rate are obtained in closed form, exactly along a one-dimensional channel; with the sum rule they give a single exponential, a lower bound on the ISF. Exact relaxation spectra show that this single exponential, built from equilibrium quantities alone, reproduces the correlation time only at weak coupling; only there can a measured dephasing rate be read through the statics. For strong attraction the slow dissolution of clusters lengthens the correlation time up to ninefold, whereas a repulsive layer relaxes nearly as a single mode at its structure peak. The dephasing rate, fitted over a time window where it is nearly constant, approaches the slowest relaxation rate, not the initial one; the sum rule reaches the data only through the area beneath the normalized decay, which defines the correlation time. For CO/Cu(001), the lattice gas reproduces the dephasing rates measured along two crystal directions and requires a nearest-neighbour repulsion of at least $ 35$ –$ 50$ ~meV, whatever the jump rates; the measured rate is then close to the inverse correlation time, and the memory is predicted to grow with coverage.

Cooperation of itinerant and localized physics in the magnetic phase diagram of 214 Ruddlesden-Popper iridates

New Submission | Strongly Correlated Electrons (cond-mat.str-el) | 2026-10-27 20:00 EDT

Francesco Cassol, Michele Casula, Benjamin Lenz

The $ 5d^5$ Ruddlesden-Popper iridates Sr$ _2$ IrO$ _4$ and Ba$ _2$ IrO$ _4$ have attracted considerable attention as spin-orbit entangled analogues of the cuprates, yet the origin of their antiferromagnetic insulating state, of Slater or of Mott-Heisenberg type, remains debated. We find that both materials sit in an intermediate regime in which itinerant and localized electrons cooperate in stabilizing the antiferromagnetic order, with Ba$ _2$ IrO$ _4$ slightly more correlated than Sr$ _2$ IrO$ 4$ . We obtain this by computing, within dynamical mean-field theory, the interaction-temperature phase diagram of a realistic three-orbital $ t{2g}$ Hubbard model with spin-orbit coupling. By placing both compounds in a common phase diagram, our results reconcile seemingly contradictory experimental and theoretical reports, and provide a unified framework for future studies of the 214 iridates and of other spin-orbit coupled correlated materials.

New Submission | Mesoscale and Nanoscale Physics (cond-mat.mes-hall) | 2026-10-27 20:00 EDT

J. H. Warnes, Dayana Joy, Pankaj Bhalla, Rhonald Burgos Atencia

We investigate the orbital Hall effect in a three-band system described by a low-energy Hamiltonian containing both linear and quadratic momentum-dependent contributions. The linear term lifts the degeneracy between the dispersive bands, providing a minimal setting to study orbital transport in a multiband system with a curved central band. Within a quantum kinetic approach, we derive the intrinsic orbital Hall response and analyze its microscopic origin in terms of interband coherence induced by an external electric field. We show that the orbital Hall conductivity arises from the interplay between the momentum-dependent orbital character of the eigenstates and transitions between different bands. The spin-1 structure provides a particularly transparent platform in which the contributions associated with different interband channels can be identified separately. We further show that the differential orbital Hall response provides a direct probe of the low-energy structure, exhibiting distinct scaling singularities associated with the linear and quadratic bands, respectively, while remaining independent of the ultraviolet cutoff. Our results establish a connection between the multiband quantum geometry of spin-1 fermions and their transverse orbital response, and provide a simple framework for investigating orbital transport beyond two-band models.

Bumseop Kim, Sayed Ali Akbar Ghorashi, Yicong Chen, Ritesh Agarwal, Andrew M. Rappe

Spatially structured light carries independent phase and envelope variations in addition to its polarization and frequency. We develop a first-principles theory of second-order dc photocurrents (especially shift and injection) that resolves how these optical degrees of freedom drive distinct dc responses in crystals. The second-order dc response couples two optical field components at the same frequency but with generally different wave vectors. Expanding the response to first order in these wave vectors identifies two rank-four material tensors: $ \xi$ , which couples to the optical phase gradient and contains the plane-wave photon-drag limit, and $ \zeta$ , which couples to the intensity gradient and describes a spatially varying dc current. Together, these results show how the spatial structure of light can serve as an independent control variable for nonlinear photocurrent generation and provide a first-principles route to predict this response in real materials.
